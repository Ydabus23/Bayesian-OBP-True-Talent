Small-Sample Adjustments in Baseball: Applying Bayesian Inference to True-Talent OBP

How much should 100 plate appearances change what we believe about a hitter? A worked Bayesian updating problem, with an interactive model. By Yusuf Dabus.

1. The problem

Suppose half of the batters in a league have a true-talent on-base percentage (OBP) of .300 and the other half have a true talent of .360. A hypothetical hitter, Marcus Hale, has reached base 42 times in 120 plate appearances, an observed OBP of .350. What is the probability that his true talent is actually .360?

The intuitive answer is “very likely,” since .350 is so close to .360. Bayes’ theorem says it is only about 66%. The remaining 34% is regression to the mean in action.

3. Method

Let T₁ = .300 and T₂ = .360 be the two talent hypotheses, and let the data D be k = 42 times on base in n = 120 PA. The binomial likelihood for each hypothesis is:

P(D | T) = C(n, k) · Tk · (1 − T)n−k

With equal priors of 0.50, the binomial coefficient and the priors cancel, leaving the likelihood ratio:

R = (0.360 / 0.300)42 · (0.640 / 0.700)78

Working in logs avoids numerical underflow: ln R = 42(0.18232) + 78(−0.08829) ≈ 0.843, so R ≈ 1.95. The posterior is then:

P(T₂ | D) = R / (1 + R) = 1.95 / 2.95 ≈ 0.661 ≈ 66%

For unequal priors, multiply R by the prior odds P(T₂)/P(T₁) before converting. The interactive model above does exactly this.

5. What it means for baseball

Regression to the mean is just Bayes. A .350 line over 120 PA is consistent with both talent levels, so the best estimate (about .340 here) is pulled below his observed .350, toward the league’s lower group.

Evidence compounds, and it is slow. Posterior odds grow multiplicatively with sample size. It takes roughly 480 PA of the same rate to reach about 93% confidence, and even 600 PA only gets to about 97%.

The prior matters most early. In April, the league-wide distribution of talent should carry more weight than a hot or cold start. This is why projection systems blend a player’s results with a population prior.

6. Limitations and extensions

Real talent is continuous, not two-point. A natural extension is a Beta-Binomial model: with a Beta(α, β) prior fit to league OBP, the posterior mean is (k + α) / (n + α + β). I also ignore park, opponent quality, and aging effects, and treat plate appearances as independent and identically distributed.

7. Code

import math

def posterior_high(k, n, p_low=0.300, p_high=0.360, prior_high=0.5):
    log_r = k * math.log(p_high / p_low) \
          + (n - k) * math.log((1 - p_high) / (1 - p_low))
    log_odds = log_r + math.log(prior_high / (1 - prior_high))
    return 1 / (1 + math.exp(-log_odds))

print(round(posterior_high(42, 120), 2))  # 0.66
